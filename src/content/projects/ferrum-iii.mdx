# Ferrum: Layer III — Hardening, Honest Baselines, and a Novelty Head

---

<br />

## Abstract

[Layer II](/projects/ferrum-ii) proved a system: raw packets travel from a NIC into GPU VRAM and a CUDA-resident MLP classifies them at line rate, no CPU in the data path. That was a *systems* result, and it stood on synthetic traffic — four trivially separable classes generated by our own sender. Good enough to prove the data path; not good enough to publish.

Layer III is the three-week sprint (2026-04-18 → 2026-05-05) that closed the gap between "it works" and "a reviewer can't dismiss it." It did three things. **Stage 1** hardened the CUDA hot path and reconciled the code against the paper's claimed numbers. **Stage 2** retrained the classifier on CIC-IDS2017 with re-derived features that match the on-device vector byte-for-byte, under a two-split evaluation that separates ordinary temporal generalization from unseen-sub-attack OOD — and ran an honest head-to-head against the one publicly reproducible MLP baseline for the dataset. **Stage 3** added a post-hoc novelty detector on the penultimate embedding, after a pre-registered sunset clause retired the originally-committed spiking-neural-network direction on evidence rather than on taste.

The systems thesis is unchanged from Layer II. What changed is that every number is now defensible, every baseline is reproducible, and every retired direction is documented instead of quietly deleted.

## The trigger: a citation audit that failed

The sprint opened with a max-effort citation audit of the Phase 7 planning document (`07_phase7_citation_audit_2026-04-18.md`). It was not a formality. Of the 12 citations in the planning doc, the audit returned:

- **4 removed as fabricated** — papers, authors, venues, and DOIs that do not exist in any canonical index. One cited a dataset paper at a hostname (`unucic.github.io`) that does not resolve.
- **1 replaced** — a fabricated "autoencoder zero-day detection, 94% / &lt;5% FP" citation, swapped for the real and canonical Kitsune (Mirsky et al., NDSS 2018); the headline numbers had been invented and were removed.
- **3 attribution-fixed** — real papers cited under the wrong authors or with fabricated accuracy/power-reduction figures appended.
- **2 verified, 1 verified-with-caveat, 1 left as an explicit TODO** where the publisher page was bot-blocked and authorship couldn't be independently confirmed.

The fabrications lived in the *planning* document — almost certainly artifacts of an earlier LLM-assisted drafting pass. The paper itself (`paper.md`) was checked in the same pass and found clean: "no shared numeric bibliography, no fabricated-author tokens found." The audit ran under a rule worth stating plainly: **publisher-canonical source is mandatory** (DOI resolver or the publisher's own landing page; ResearchGate and Google Scholar are corroborators, not authorities), and **no fabricated replacements** — when a citation turns out to be invented, either substitute a real paper that actually supports the claim or remove the citation and rewrite the prose so it no longer depends on a source that was never there.

This is the discipline the rest of Layer III runs on. A NIDS paper that cites six imaginary papers is not a paper; it's a draft with a credibility leak. Finding and sealing that leak was item zero.

## Why a staircase, and in this order

The sprint runs as three stages — hardening, then real data, then research — and the *order* is load-bearing, not incidental. The audit's eight levers don't ship a paper on their own; what ships a paper is a research claim no reviewer can wave away. So Stage 1 goes first to make the substrate sound — there's no point measuring a novel detector's latency or accuracy on a pipeline with silent drops and unreconciled headline numbers. Stage 2 goes second to make the *corpus* credible — synthetic traffic can't carry an evaluation. Stage 3 goes last, so that by the time the research claim is measured, "but the upstream pipeline had bugs" and "but the training data was synthetic" are already off the table.

One sequencing choice paid off directly: **Stage 2 carries the Stage 3 de-risk pilot.** The QCFS sunset-clause Phase 1 ran *inside* the Stage 2 retraining pipeline, against Stage-2-quality data — so when [the pivot fired](/logbook/2026-04-20-ferrum-iii-the-pivot), it fired before any expensive Stage 3 integration had been committed. That's a large part of why the pivot was cheap.

## Stage 1 — Hardening the hot path

Stage 1 was eight numbered items against the CUDA receiver — every one a *bug fix*, not a new feature, none glamorous, all load-bearing. They ran as Wave A (items 1–3, independent and parallelizable) and Wave B (items 4–7, serialized because they all touch the same one or two files), with item 8 last against the post-Wave-B substrate:

- **Items 1–2** — persist train-time min/max normalization parameters into the model binary, and move the MLP weights into `__constant__` memory (where the classify kernel reads them with broadcast latency).
- **Item 3** — pin `nvcc` to `-arch=sm_61 -lineinfo -Xptxas -v` so the build targets Pascal correctly and surfaces register pressure.
- **Items 4–7** — correctness fixes in `classify_flows` and the flow table: early-out on empty slots, a `missed_flow_count` atomic so dropped flows are counted rather than silently lost, TTL-based flow-table eviction at the classify tick, and response-ring counter bounds.
- **Item 8** — a benchmark harness plus a numeric audit of `paper.md`. The headline numbers had drifted between `INDEX.md`, `02_phases.md`, and the paper; item 8 reconciled them against the checked-in CSVs and retired the stale figures, so there is now one source of truth for every number the paper claims.

Stage 1 is the least quotable stage and the one that makes the rest trustworthy: a paper's evaluation section is only as good as the instrumentation underneath it.

## Stage 2 — Real data, two splits, and an honest baseline

### From synthetic to CIC-IDS2017

Layer II's 99.8% accuracy was on synthetic traffic the sender itself generated — clean, separable, and meaningless as a generalization claim (the pre-Stage-2 corpus was ~20 true samples per class after a `pkt_count ≥ 100` filter). Stage 2 retrains the same `15 → 64 → 32 → 4` MLP on CIC-IDS2017 (Sharafaldin et al., 2018), with the 15 features re-derived from raw PCAPs to match the on-device feature vector byte-for-byte. Fifteen raw sub-attacks collapse to a 4-class taxonomy — Benign, DoS/DDoS, Probe, and Brute-force/Exploit. That coarseness is deliberate: it's the finest cut the 15 header-only features (packet/byte counts, timing, TCP-flag distributions — no payload, no entropy, no SNI) can defensibly support, framed in the paper as an information-theoretic limit rather than a feature-engineering deficit. Heartbleed and Infiltration are held out of *every* split as a zero-day class with no parent in the taxonomy.

### Splits that don't lie: three generalization claims

A single day-based split would conflate three distinct questions the CIC-IDS2017 literature routinely runs together (a conflation Engelen et al., 2021, flag directly). Stage 2 separates them:

- **Split A — ordinary temporal generalization.** Within each session capture, every sub-attack and benign stream is sorted by first-packet timestamp and partitioned 80/10/10 train/val/test. Every class appears in all three; the test window just comes later in wall-clock time. The regime the shipped model actually runs in.
- **Split B — unseen sub-attack within a known class.** Holds specific sub-attacks (DDoS-LOIT, Web-Attack BF+XSS, Bot) out of training — the model has seen the parent super-class but never that sub-attack.
- **Zero-day holdout — unseen attack family.** Heartbleed and Infiltration appear in no split at all and have no parent class. Strictly stronger than Split B.

The point of the structure is that the failure modes differ: a Split A miss would be an overfitting bug, while Split B and zero-day misses are the OOD problem Stage 3 exists to solve — at the sub-attack and family level respectively. Carrying the falsifiable novelty claim in the *split design*, not just the algorithm, is itself a contribution.

Split A clears the pre-registered macro-F1 target of 0.88:

| Class | Precision | Recall | F1 | Support |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Benign | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 152,498 |
| DoS/DDoS | 0.97 | 1.00 | 0.99 | 5,953 |
| Probe | 0.99 | 0.99 | 0.99 | 15,868 |
| Brute-force/Exploit | 0.60 | 0.63 | 0.61 | 1,093 |
| **Macro** | 0.89 | 0.90 | **0.90** | — |

The Brute-force/Exploit class misses all three of its per-class targets (F1 0.61 vs target 0.77), and the paper reports it as a real gap rather than iterating the training loop until it disappears. The cause is geometric, not a bug: that one coarse class bundles five sub-attacks whose packet-count medians span 4–22 and durations span 0.09–12 s — no single 15-dimensional decision surface separates that heterogeneous cluster from Benign.

Split B collapses to macro-F1 **0.37** — DoS/DDoS and Brute-force/Exploit both hit *zero recall*. Friday's DDoS-LOIT (median 8 packets, 10 ms, SYN-heavy) is classified as Probe; Bot (median 4 packets) is classified as Benign. This is the intrinsic ceiling of header-only 4-class classification against sub-attacks outside the training set — and it's the result that motivates Stage 3.

### The §5.5 head-to-head: choosing a baseline you can't be dunked on

The one openly reproducible MLP baseline for CIC-IDS2017 is MLP4NIDS (Rosay et al., 2021), MIT-licensed. We re-implemented it in PyTorch and trained it on *our* 15-feature Split A under Rosay's published hyperparameters:

| | ferrum-cic-v1 (shipped) | MLP4NIDS (rerun) | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Architecture | 15→64→32→4 ReLU | 15→256→256→4 SELU+Dropout | — |
| Parameters | 3,236 | 70,916 | **21.9×** |
| Training wall (P40) | ≈9 min | ≈54 min | 6.0× |
| **Test macro-F1** | **0.896** | **0.925** | **+0.029** |
| — Brute-force/Exploit F1 | 0.613 | 0.715 | **+0.102** |

MLP4NIDS wins by +0.029 macro-F1, with the gap concentrated on the minority class. We report that loss plainly. The point of the comparison isn't "we win" — it's *positioning*: a larger closed-set MLP captures ~10 points of minority-class F1 that the on-device model does not, but it carries 22× the parameters and would not fit in the GPU constant memory the line-rate kernel depends on. The choice between them is a deployment-budget choice, with MLP4NIDS as the honest reference point above the budget line. A reviewer can reproduce the baseline; the temporal-leakage caveat is cited (Engelen 2021); the gap is disclosed. That's a baseline that survives review.

## Stage 3 — A novelty head, after a pivot paid for in advance

### The pivot

Stage 3's research contribution was originally committed to a **spiking neural network** (SNN) — the high-novelty angle, with a sharp originality ceiling because no prior work occupies Ferrum's exact substrate. That commitment was bound, *before any code ran*, to a falsifiable sunset clause: a QCFS-activation variant of the MLP would be trained on the same Stage 2 splits, and if it dropped more than 5 points of macro-F1 (or other pre-registered triggers), Stage 3 would pivot to novelty/OOD detection — but only after a mandatory two-phase floor (a hyperparameter grid sweep, then a structural-variant retry) to distinguish "wrong knobs" from "wrong angle."

It missed. Phase 1's best of five configurations landed macro-F1 0.547 against the ANN's 0.896 (−34.9 pp). Phase 2 ran the sunset-clause-official hybrid-init variant plus a five-variant supplementary portfolio; five of six missed the same way, and the one that cleared accuracy (post-hoc threshold-balancing at T=128) did so at 128× the GEMM count — a latency miss instead of an accuracy miss. The clause fired. Stage 3 pivoted to novelty/OOD. The full methodology of that pivot is its own story — see the [logbook entry on the pivot](/logbook/2026-04-20-ferrum-iii-the-pivot).

### Mahalanobis++ on the penultimate embedding

The deployed novelty head scores the *frozen* classifier with no retraining. Four detectors were evaluated on the 32-D penultimate embedding; the headline is the one corpus where the closed-set softmax is not already saturating:

| corpus | MSP | Maha++-ℓ₂ | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| zero-day holdout (7 flows) | 0.5575 | **0.9746** | **+41.7 pp** (z = +4.24) |
| test_ddos (Friday LOIT) | 0.731 | **0.925** | **+19.5 pp** |

On the Friday DDoS-LOIT variant the closed-set head confidently mislabels as Probe, Mahalanobis++-ℓ₂ lifts AUROC from 0.73 to 0.93. **Energy (Liu et al., 2020) was rejected** — it under-performs the free max-softmax baseline on every corpus tested, for a structural reason the paper isolates: the final layer's null space is 29-dimensional, and the OOD signal lives largely in the component Energy discards (corroborated by retraining with LogitNorm, which suppresses the logit-magnitude pathway yet leaves a 29-point gap). Three independent detector families (ViM, GradNorm, DeepSVDD) and a 3-seed replication confirm the Mahalanobis result is not selection bias.

The head costs **4,628 bytes** of constant memory (per-class means + a pooled precision matrix) on top of the 13 KB of MLP weights, and gates only the post-MLP distance block — so the argmax path is byte-identical with the detector on or off. Live-validated through the Mellanox DAC pair at saturation (4.8 Mpps sustained), the end-to-end overhead is **+6.06 µs mean / +3.24 µs p99** — comfortably inside the pre-registered 2× latency budget. The deployment claim: *first NIDS computing a penultimate-Mahalanobis OOD signal entirely on a NIC-fed GPU, fused into the same kernel that produces the prediction, under a microsecond marginal budget.*

## Retired directions

The paper keeps a short section (§7.4) on the SNN attempt rather than deleting it. Two conversion paths were tried on the shared topology — direct rate-coded ANN→SNN over a timestep sweep, and QCFS with hybrid ANN-initialized training — and the pre-registration fixed an accuracy floor at Phase 2. QCFS missed it; the sunset clause retired SNN from the paper. The P40-CUDA substrate is substrate-agnostic and would accept an SNN or any other CUDA-expressible model if a future conversion clears the bar. Reporting the attempt is the point: the sunset clause is *what the pre-registration was for*, and it worked as intended.

## Where Layer III sits

[Layer I](/projects/ferrum-i) proved mirrored switch traffic reaches a sensor faithfully. [Layer II](/projects/ferrum-ii) proved a GPU can classify it at line rate with the CPU out of the path. Layer III makes the classifier on top of that substrate *defensible*: trained on real data, measured under splits that don't flatter it, benchmarked against a reproducible baseline, and extended with a novelty head whose every rejected alternative is on the record. The contribution remains, as the paper says, "primarily on the systems side" — Layer III is what it took to be able to say that and have it hold up.

The paper targets a 2027 cycle; four venues are in contention (RAID as the canonical fit, with NDSS / USENIX Security re-opened now that the security-venue path the SNN commit had foreclosed is restored).
